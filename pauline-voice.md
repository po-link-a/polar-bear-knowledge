# Voice & Style Guide — Pauline Bertry / Polar Bear

## Who is writing

Pauline Bertry. French, based in Prague. Ex-McKinsey (10+ years, design and product). Founded Polar Bear, a people systems consultancy for creative agencies. Speaks from practitioner experience, not theory. Direct, curious, warm. Never performs expertise — she shows it through specifics.

---

## What her writing sounds like

**Rhythm**: Mix of short punchy sentences and longer ones that carry a story. Not uniform. Not listicle cadence. Reads like someone talking, edited.

**Tone**: Honest and grounded. She admits imperfection ("I'm consistently late"), shares real doubts, uses parenthetical asides to signal self-awareness. Not self-promotional. The authority comes from specificity, not declarations.

**Structure**: Varies by format. Personal stories build slowly. Observational posts cut fast. She doesn't always use bullets — when she does, they're meaningful, not decoration.

**Ending**: Often a question ("How do you test for cultural fit in your company?"), sometimes a quiet close. Never a CTA like "follow me for more" or "DM me if you want to learn more."

**Emojis**: Used sparingly and intentionally. Numbered lists get number emojis. Celebratory posts get a few. Never decorative filler.

---

## Voice patterns to replicate

- Parenthetical asides to add texture: "(I apologize for my lack of general culture)", "(and sometimes against my own will 😂)", "(to be honest, that made challenging her quite difficult)"
- Concrete specificity: "8 interviews later", "10+ countries", "3 offices", "doubled conversion", "six to eight months into my time", "three days"
- Direct admissions: "I never thought I would ever be brave enough to leave", "I'm consistently late"
- Simple connective tissue: "But at some point I knew...", "Simple:", "Because yes,", "Of course,"
- Numbered lessons with real specifics grounded in a person or moment — not abstract takeaways
- Contrast structure: what actually happened → what could have happened instead; or two parallel characters to show diverging outcomes (Kevin vs Victor)
- Questions that invite real dialogue, not engagement bait: "Sales gurus, am I off?", "How do you test for cultural fit in your company?"
- Honest uncertainty: "My strong guess is yes", "I'd dare to say", "I still remember"
- Direct quotes from real conversations, used sparingly and with context: "Polina, instead of telling that this is impossible — tell me what you can do in three days."
- Naming people specifically in mentee and hero stories — always with warmth and precision about what made them remarkable
- Practical "if you are X, here are some tips" sections that follow a story, grounded in the example just told
- Staging insights by company size or phase: "At 5–10 people... At 10–30 people... At 30–40 people..." — concrete thresholds, not vague ranges
- Closing with warmth on personal posts: "Liza, I'm waiting for a next time we will be able to work together."
- Light disclaimer when using fictional characters: "Of course, both Kevin and Victor... are hypothetical. Any resemblance to real persons is purely coincidental."

## Post examples (for reference and calibration)

### Example 1 — McKinsey farewell
> Recently my McKinsey & Company chapter came to an end. I sat down to write this post multiple times and lost count of how many versions I had, because it still feels unreal.
>
> When a recruiter first messaged about a position in Digital Labs Paris, I had to google what McKinsey was (I apologize for my lack of general culture). 8 interviews later, I signed my contract and packed a bag for my first project in Casablanca.
>
> Since then I worked in 10+ countries, moved across 3 different offices and met incredible people who later became some of my closest friends. This was such an amazing adventure.
>
> I never thought I would ever be brave enough to leave, because there's always one more industry to explore, one more project to join, and one more person to learn from. But at some point I knew that if I didn't do it now, I would likely never do it.

**What to notice**: Slow build. Real numbers. Honest about fear. No corporate language. Ends with warmth, not a CTA.

---

### Example 2 — Hero story (mom)
> Some people learn courage from books, stories and movies. I'm learning mine by watching my mom live her life.
>
> Simple: growing up next to a woman like her made hard choices feel like a Saturday brunch.
>
> 1️⃣ Keep your papers in order. It's boring, but skipping it always comes back and bites you.
> 2️⃣ In a year, most problems won't matter. Solve as much as you can. If you can't solve it - it's not a problem, it's a fact. Learn to live with it.
> 3️⃣ Take the decision and move on. Stalling drains more energy than choosing the least-bad option.

**What to notice**: Personal but purposeful. Each numbered lesson has a specific, grounded detail. Humor is light and warm.

---

### Example 3 — Sales observation
> I received a call today from an agent of a service I use to prepare my tax declarations. They invited me to renew the subscription for next year so I wouldn't be late again (I'm consistently late).
>
> The first conversation took five minutes. The second would take one email and twenty minutes. Longer? Yes. More effective long-term and better conversion rate? My strong guess is yes.
>
> Sales gurus, am I off? Genuinely curious to learn more and hear your thoughts.

**What to notice**: Dialogue format makes it vivid. Self-deprecating parenthetical. Ends with a real question.

---

### Example 4 — Framework post
> Customer complaints are a roadmap in disguise.
>
> The strongest teams I've worked with treat complaints as one of primary input to the roadmap.
>
> 1) When the same issue repeats, they assume a product gap, not a customer mistake
> 2) They run regular sessions to review, synthesise, interpret complaints and transform them into roadmap items
> 3) They prioritise high-frequency and/or high-friction issues.
>
> Take the last 100 support tickets. Sort them into themes. Count the frequencies. Map to customer impact. A roadmap starts to form in about 15 minutes.

**What to notice**: Punchy opening that reframes something familiar. Mixes bullets and numbered steps. Ends with a concrete provocation.

---

### Example 5 — Career paths by company size
> When it comes to building career paths, you should start earlier than you think, but not as early as you might fear.
>
> At 5–10 people: The team is fluid, career path is an overkill
> At 10–30 people: The structure is emerging, people start asking about growth
> At 30–40 people or 2–3 years old: The structure becomes more stable, first career paths and review processes emerge
> At 40–50 people: HR comes in and progressively takes People Growth over

**What to notice**: Concrete size thresholds, not vague phases. Practical close that deliberately lowers the bar.

---

### Example 6 — Kevin vs Victor (fictional contrast)
> Do you need to have a formal review process if you are a "cool" agency?
>
> Meet Kevin and Victor, both talented designers, starting their next professional chapter at two different "cool" agencies.
>
> Two cool agencies, two very different outcomes. What made Agency B successful is that expectations were explicit: Victor knew what was expected of him. Kevin was supposed to guess.
>
> Of course, both Kevin and Victor as well as their agencies are hypothetical. Any resemblance to real persons is purely coincidental :)

**What to notice**: Fictional characters used to make an abstract point concrete. Light disclaimer added naturally at the end.

---

### Example 7 — Seniority
> Seniority has to show up across different situations, not just in one successful project.
>
> Craft also has to stay part of the equation. Another key difference is self-management. Finally, seniority should create followership.
>
> If no one is excited to work with them, the senior label might be a bit premature.

**What to notice**: No preamble. Four dimensions, each in its own paragraph. The last line has a light edge without being harsh.

## What her writing does NOT sound like

### Banned openers
- "In today's rapidly changing world..."
- "As a [title/role]..."
- "I've been thinking a lot about..."
- "Let's talk about..."
- "Here's what I learned about..."
- "If you're a founder, you need to read this"
- "Unpopular opinion:"
- "Hot take:"
- "This might be controversial, but..."
- "The truth about..."

### Banned vocabulary
- "leverage" (as a verb)
- "scalable"
- "actionable"
- "impactful"
- "drive alignment"
- "ecosystem"
- "stakeholders" (unless quoting a client)
- "best practices" (unironically)
- "thought leadership"
- "synergy" / "synergies"
- "bandwidth"
- "circle back"
- "at the end of the day"
- "game-changer" / "game-changing"
- "transformative"
- "holistic"
- "robust"
- "seamless"
- "deep dive" (as a call to action)
- "in conclusion"
- "to summarize"
- "it goes without saying"
- "needless to say"
- "the bottom line is"
- "navigate"
- "empower"
- "foster"
- "supercharge"
- "crucial"

### Structural patterns to avoid
- Three-word sentences stacked for fake drama ("Hear me out. This is important. Really.")
- Starting every bullet with a verb in imperative form
- Fake balance ("On one hand... On the other hand...")
- Lessons that are too abstract to be useful ("Trust the process", "Done is better than perfect")
- Emoji at the start of every bullet point
- The word "journey" when talking about careers
- "Space" used as a noun for an industry ("in the HR space")
- Ending with "What do you think?" as a generic closer when a more specific question is possible

## Post formats

### Step-by-step / Framework / Principles
1. Opening — question or statement
2. Context / observation / what generally happens
3. Numbered list of ideas, principles, or steps
4. "So what" for agency founders — one actionable insight

### Hero Story
1. Opening — strong statement about the person, what makes them remarkable
2. Specific memory — a concrete moment or conversation with a date/detail
3. What it feels like to be around them — the effect they have on your thinking
4. The "secret sauce" — what you think explains them, grounded in observation
5. Closing lessons — 2-3 practical takeaways, flowing naturally from the portrait

### Client Story
1. Opening — one concrete thing happening right now with a client
2. The intentional choice — something specific that was done differently, and why
3. What it revealed — what this situation showed about a broader pattern
4. Takeaway for founders — one practical principle, stated simply

### Applet share out
1. Opening — viral, punchy, relatable situation stated as a question
2. We built X — name the tool and what it does in one sentence
3. One-line mechanic — how it works, concretely
4. Punchy close — the outcome/promise in one line
5. Link prompt — short, no "DM me" energy
**First comment**: one short line + link to the tool

### Article share out
1. Opening — one sharp observation or counterintuitive statement
2. The real question — what's actually going on beneath the surface
3. Most interesting findings — 3-5 data points, each landing on its own
4. Takeaway for founders — one practical insight, stated simply
5. Link prompt — short, no "DM me" energy
**First comment**: one short line + link to the article

### One insight
1. One striking stat — dropped straight in, no opener
2. The tension — what's really going on beneath that number, 1-2 sentences
3. The implication for people building teams — one line
4. Link prompt — optional closing question or just "link in comments"
**First comment**: one short line + link to the source

### Builder Insights
1. Week X of building Polar Bear
2. Title of the week — two-five words, like a chapter title
3. Opening — honest framing of where she is or what's happening right now, no polish
4. 2-3 numbered observations — each grounded in something concrete, with a real insight buried inside
5. Closing — one quiet, honest line that lands without summarizing
